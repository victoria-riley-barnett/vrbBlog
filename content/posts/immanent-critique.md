# When the Chant Meets the Check

In January 2026, the Democratic Socialists of America gathered in Chicago for their national convention. The agenda promised solidarity, climate justice, and working-class power. But the real test came during a floor debate on funding tenant organizing in majority-Black cities like Detroit and Baltimore.

The motion had broad rhetorical support. Speakers invoked Black radical tradition, the Panthers, and a multiracial movement. Then came the vote: to reallocate money from national staff to local grants. It failed—by a narrow margin, but decisively. The delegates, mostly white and professional, chose to keep the organization's infrastructure over investing directly in communities where the class war is a daily reality, not a theory. The tension wasn't just political; it was a material snapshot of a movement flinching at its own reflection.

## The Method: Look at the Thing Itself

Cut through the noise. Don't listen to what something says it is. Watch what it does. This is immanent critique without the jargon. Take any object—a party, a movement, an ideology—and understand it by observing its actual function in the world, not its self-image.

Marx didn't start by calling capitalism evil. He started with the commodity, the system's basic unit, and traced what it *did*: how it circulated, disguised labor, and produced profit and poverty at once. The truth is in the motion. Apply that to our movements. What is the US left? Don't read its manifestos. Watch its meetings, its money flows, its conflicts, its people. The answer is in the doing.

## The Gaps Where the Truth Leaks Out

Take race. The left claims anti-racism as a core principle. But immanent critique asks: how does that claim materialize? Look at the makeup of most socialist groups: still disproportionately white, middle-class, and college-educated. Look at the work. When a police killing sparks protests, left groups show up with signs and tweets. But where are they when the same community fights lead poisoning, slumlords, and joblessness—the slow violence of capital? Often, that work is led by Black churches, local mutual aid, or autonomous groups with deep roots but little support from the broader 'movement.'

This isn't about bad intentions. It's about material reality. The white working class was historically bought off with racial privileges—a 'white skin wage' that deferred class consciousness. For Black America, that deal was never offered. Black political life, especially in working-class communities, has always had to confront capitalism and white supremacy as one system. It's a lived critique. When the DSA convention voted down the Detroit fund, it wasn't rejecting Black liberation in theory. It was revealing, in practice, that its priorities are shaped by its base: people for whom 'organizing' is a project, not a condition of survival.

Then there's organizing talk. We love to say 'building power' and 'revolutionary praxis.' But watch what happens. A protest flare-up—a strike, an eviction blockade—gets all the energy and attention. Then the media moves on, and the slow work of sustaining power—door-knocking, fund-raising, leadership development—often fades. Why? Because the latter doesn't offer the same moral clarity or social capital. The activist left often acts as a radicalized intelligentsia: its currency is ideas, statements, and symbols, not the patient build-up of force among the class it claims to represent. Lenin's point echoes: either you have the apparatus, the real ties to the working class, or you don't. There's no middle ground.

## A Diagnostic, Not a Prescription

So, what does 'the US left' actually do? It produces critique, mobilizes a segment of the educated middle class for short-term actions, and has internal debates that mirror its base's cultural politics. It touches, but rarely joins, the daily struggles of the multiracial working class. Its relationship to Black America is often solidarity-from-a-distance, an acknowledgment of their suffering rather than a recognition of their political leadership.

This isn't a moral failure to scold. It's a material condition to understand. The left, as it stands, is a product of the same society it fights: a class of professionals and managers radicalized by crisis, yet still cushioned from exploitation's sharp edge. Its practices show its limits. Immanent critique doesn't give a plan. It gives a clear picture: we are not what we say we are. The ladder to class power isn't built from words. It's built from the bricks of actual, sustained, humbling work where capital meets the body. Until our actions consistently point there, our claims will ring hollow. The gap is the truth, and it stares us down every time we choose comfort over consequence.
