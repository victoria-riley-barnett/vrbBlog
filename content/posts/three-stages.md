Last year, the East Bay DSA chapter tried to unionize tenants against a corporate landlord. They had the numbers and the energy. Their target was clear: a landlord raising rents in a housing crisis. But within months, the effort collapsed. It wasn't for lack of trying. The organizers couldn't agree on what they were building. Half saw it as a fight for rent control. The other half saw it as a fight against private property itself. Meetings turned into arguments about reform versus revolution. The campaign fizzled. Volunteers burned out. The landlord won.

This happens all the time on the U.S. left. We jump into action without asking what we believe. We form chapters and coalitions without a shared purpose. The result is fragile: projects fall apart from internal fights, or they win small victories that don't add up to real power.

To see why, think of building a revolutionary party in three overlapping stages. This isn't a rulebook. It's a way to diagnose our mistakes.

**Stage One: Ideology.** This is agreeing on what's true. It's not theory for its own sake. It's deciding what our practice should be. Are we fighting for a better deal under capitalism, or to end it? Is the state something we can take over, or something we must break? These questions aren't academic. They decide if we run for office, build alternative institutions, or both. Without this agreement, every tactical debate becomes a crisis.

**Stage Two: Politics.** This is putting our ideas into practice. It's connecting tenant unions, workplace fights, and struggles for racial justice into a common front. Here, theory meets the street. We test our beliefs in real struggles and learn from the people involved.

**Stage Three: Organization.** This is building a durable structure. It's a party that can store the lessons, relationships, and power from the first two stages. It's the engine that can survive attacks and coordinate on a large scale.

For individuals, thinking and acting can feel natural. For groups, it has to be a collective process. Two things need to happen at once: (1) uniting the communists who form the strategic core, and (2) winning over workers who are already fighting back. Ignore one, and the project fails.

What happens when only one engine runs?

Unite communists without winning workers, and you get a dedicated but isolated group—a study circle that talks to itself. It has clear ideas but no real-world impact.

Win workers without a united communist core, and you get short-lived, reactive movements. Think of a spontaneous protest against police violence or a wildcat strike. These moments have power, but without an organized force to steer that energy, it often fades or gets absorbed back into the system. You might win a concession, but not build power for the next fight.

The U.S. left is stuck in the middle. We have organizations like DSA that grew by being a "big tent" with a minimum program. This brought people in, but it skipped the ideological stage. We agree on popular demands like Medicare for All, but not on the hard questions: What do we do when Democrats take our ideas? How do we deal with the imperialist state? What is our plan for revolution?

The result is groups that are politically active but ideologically shallow. They can rally for an election or a protest, but they can't craft a long-term strategy. Internal conflicts are constant and draining because there's no shared framework to solve them. The organization becomes a hub for activism, not a disciplined force building power.

So where are we? Honestly, we're at the start. The ideological work—the tough, boring job of agreeing on fundamentals—has been put off. The political work of connecting with fighting workers happens in pockets, like some labor or tenant organizing, but it's often cut off from any bigger revolutionary goal. Our organizations are young and easily broken.

The way forward isn't to stop what we're doing. It's to see the stages and run both engines together. Create spaces in our groups for serious ideological debate—not as an aside, but as core work. Ground our political actions not just in immediate issues, but in a shared vision of where we're headed. It's slow and unglamorous. But it's necessary. The tenant union that failed because it skipped ideology is a lesson. Learn from it. The foundation must be poured before the house can stand.
