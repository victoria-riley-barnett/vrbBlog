I designed and teach CSC 411, an interdisciplinary machine learning course at San Francisco State University. I plan to release the course materials under a permissive license sometime following the end of the semester.

The course is designed to generalize critical representation, computing complexity, and model evaluation concepts to non-computer scientists as part of the Promoting Inclusivity in Computing [(PINC)](https://www.sfsu.edu/departments/promoting-inclusivity-computing) initiative:

> The Promoting Inclusivity In Computing (PINC) program started in 2017, and at the time was designed to achieve one initiative, to provide a pathway for students majoring in the Life Sciences to learn to code and earn a minor in Computing Applications. Since then, PINC has grown to include student certificate, scholarship, summer coding, mentor, and professional certificate programs. - [San Francisco State University](https://www.sfsu.edu/departments/promoting-inclusivity-computing)

The official course title is CSC 411: Intermediate Machine Learning for Interdisciplinary Data Scientists. It is the second to last course in the minor sequence, positioned between the most data science intensive course of the sequence (CSC 408: Machine Learning and Data Science for Personalized Medicine) and the most computer science intensive (CSC 511: Protein Modeling with Deep Learning). It aims to bridge the gap between the two by following this general scheme:

1. A lecture series that follows the historical development of machine perception.
2. Labs that begin with no libraries at all, and build up to higher abstractions using Lightning and PyTorch throughout the semester.
3. Tying (1) and (2) together across a more traditional architectural and key-algorithmic retrospective;
4. Doing (3), to the maximum extent possible, in a domain-specific way that suits the cohort, ideally building bridged engineering thinking for architecting frontier applied ml specifically;
5. And capstoning the course with a final project that requires explicit sequence analysis.

In general, the goal of my course rewrite was to create a more accessible and practical learning experience for scientists who want to apply the latest in machine learning to their research, allowing them to rapidly generalize these concepts so they can get more out of the final course in the sequence: an extremely rich transformers, PCA, and alphafold course lead by a Genentech Principal.
