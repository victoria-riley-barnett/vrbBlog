The US left exists in a strategic impasse between labor reformism, Democratic Party realignment, and revolutionary focus. This impasse is not merely tactical but reflects a deeper ideological misleadership rooted in the middle-class character of its organized forces. Both labor and Democratic alignment strategies mistake themselves for oppositional projects while existing in an already-sublated state to capital—they operate within false distinctions that capital itself has superseded.

## Vanguard as Function, Not Sect

Lenin’s concept of the vanguard is frequently misunderstood as either sectarian proclamation or mass inclusion. In *What Is To Be Done?*, the vanguard is a practical function: the organized force capable of turning discontent across social strata into revolutionary opposition against the state. It is neither an ideological badge nor a demographic claim. Contemporary misuses—whether Trotskyist sectarianism or reformist “mass politics”—both evade this functional definition, substituting organizational vanity for revolutionary capacity.

The vanguard exists where struggle is protagonized, but remains insufficiently cohered. Dozens of distinct struggles mobilize organizers against the present order, yet operate separately, aligning around minimum demands rather than integrating demands into revolutionary opposition. This fragmentation has no hope of challenging the most entrenched capitalist empire.

## Middle-Class Misleadership

The organized left in the US, exemplified by formations like the Democratic Socialists of America, overwhelmingly represents a radicalized intelligentsia from the “downwardly mobile” middle strata. This class position generates specific ideological distortions: a fixation on policy reforms as incremental progress, an anxious distance from the besieged sectors of the working class, and a belief that the existing political apparatus can be realigned toward socialism.

These distortions are not personal failings but structural. The middle-class left experiences its radicalization as a choice—a realization of systemic failure—rather than as imposed by immediate material necessity. Consequently, its strategies remain within the horizon of bourgeois politics: lobbying for labor law reform, supporting progressive Democrats, building nonprofit advocacy. Each accepts the fundamental separations—between politics and economics, between race and class, between domestic and imperial struggle—that capital has already rendered obsolete.

Labor reformism and Democratic realignment are not alternative roads to socialism; they are sublated forms of capitalist politics. They operate within distinctions (worker/employer, citizen/state, national/international) that globalized, financialized capital has functionally erased. To pursue them is to fight on a field capital has already vacated.

## Socializing External Links

If the emancipation of the American working class requires the abolition of all false distinctions—race, nation, gender, legal status—then the revolutionary task is to socialize the links *external* to the middle-class left’s self-conception. This means building organizational capacity that connects the struggles capital actively suppresses: the defense of immigrants, the fight against racist policing, the solidarity with anti-imperialist movements abroad.

These are not “special interests” to be added to a class-reductionist program. They are the actual fronts of class struggle where the state’s violence is most direct and where the working class is already in motion. A vanguard that cannot protagonize these struggles cannot claim to represent the class.

The middle-class left’s anxiety about its composition—its guilt over not being “working-class enough”—is itself a symptom of its ideological captivity. The solution is not to become something else but to cease acting as if the separations it believes in are real. The party that must emerge will do so by abandoning the false choice between labor reformism and Democratic realignment, and by consciously organizing across the fractures capital exploits.

Unity must be achieved by driving everyone whose political consciousness has developed to a point of opposition with the bourgeois state into vanguard operation. This is the necessary precondition that liberal frameworks—alliances around single issues, temporary coalitions, NGO-style advocacy—fail to meet. The vanguard is not a theoretical abstraction but the practical organization of those already in struggle against the state: immigrants fighting deportation, prisoners organizing against mass incarceration, tenants resisting eviction, workers striking against exploitation, anti-imperialists confronting genocide.

These struggles are not parallel tracks to be “supported” by socialists; they are the actual sites where revolutionary opposition is being forged. The task is to build an organization that can integrate them, turning disparate defensive actions into a coordinated offensive against the capitalist state. Without this conscious effort to unite those already protagonizing struggle, the left will remain trapped in its strategic impasse, fighting battles capital has already won.
